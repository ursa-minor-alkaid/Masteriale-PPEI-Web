01Everyone tells you to be careful

The gap between fear and action

Health authorities repeat the same warning every day: beware of mosquitoes. A single bite can mean weeks of high fever and severe joint pain. The official advice—long sleeves, repellent, window screens—is all correct. But it never answers the next question: careful for how long? Mosquitoes breed everywhere, and no one can live inside a mosquito net. When "being careful" becomes a permanent burden with no way to know whether you have already been infected, the warning slowly loses its power.

A small A-BITE questionnaire (213 respondents across France, China, Vietnam and other regions) captures the gap: nearly 85% would like a testing device nearby, yet over 20% worry about hygiene and shared equipment. People are told to be careful, but have no way to find out whether they are already infected. This uncertainty is the biggest blind spot in public-health communication today.

02But how are we supposed to know?

No answer in the critical window

Knowing you should be careful is one thing; knowing whether you are already infected is another. When fever, joint pain and a rash follow a mosquito bite, the obvious question is: dengue or chikungunya? Yet no combination of symptoms can reliably tell the two diseases apart.

Both illnesses begin in the same way: sudden high fever, headache, muscle pain and rash. The differences emerge only later—and they call for different care. So in the first days, the window that matters most, patients and doctors can only guess and wait for laboratory results. More unsettling still, roughly a quarter to a half of infected people never develop symptoms at all, unknowingly passing the virus on.

03Two look-alike diseases are spreading

Same mosquito, same season, same map

Dengue and chikungunya are among the fastest-spreading mosquito-borne diseases in the world. Dengue infects an estimated 390 million people each year (WHO, 2025); chikungunya has reached more than 100 countries, and France alone reported 809 cases in 2025. The two viruses share the same mosquito, the same regions and the same seasons—health systems must track both at once, with tools that are too slow for either.

Research adds one crucial detail: a bite deposits far more virus into the skin than once assumed, and the skin begins multiplying it within hours—while the virus reaches detectable levels in the blood only one to two days later. It is this skin-to-blood time lag that makes every blood-based test start late.

04As the only real test takes days

Every shortcut still ends in a blood draw

The standard confirmation test, PCR, is accurate—but it requires a fully equipped laboratory and trained technicians. From sampling to report, the process typically takes 24–72 hours, and far longer where resources are limited. Antibody tests are simpler, but unreliable in the first days of illness—and they only reveal past exposure, not a current infection.

Rapid test strips can give an answer in 15–30 minutes, but they miss about one case in five and exist only for dengue. All of these tests require blood, which is a barrier in itself. And even when a result is finally produced, it stays inside a hospital record—never feeding the public outbreak map in real time.

05The outbreak is mapped days after it starts

The first red dot arrives a week late

Outbreak surveillance today runs on confirmed cases: a patient falls ill, seeks care, is tested, and the result climbs step by step onto the national map. By the time the first case appears there, the virus has usually been spreading silently for 5–7 days. Mild or untested cases stretch the delay further. Epidemiologists call this gap the reporting lag—the core reason every outbreak response arrives late.

Faster methods do exist in the research literature—portable tests that deliver a result in under an hour—but they remain small-scale pilots, not a routine, wide-coverage surveillance network.

A-BITE's "testing-as-surveillance" vision is designed to close exactly this gap: every anonymised test result automatically becomes a data point on the outbreak map. The moment a result is generated, it is already a surveillance signal—compressing detection delay from days down to hours.
