"Would you use a device near your home that could quickly tell you whether you have dengue or chikungunya?" We collected 213 questionnaire responses across France, China, Vietnam and other regions; 175 were complete and valid.

The answer is a qualified yes. 44.2% said yes outright, and 40.1% added "but not in my building"—84.3% in total would use it, just not inside their own home. Their top concerns—hygiene (24.8%), vandalism (20%), shared equipment (18.1%)—became three direct design decisions: a single-use disposable cartridge, a tamper-resistant wall-mounted reader, and a sterile personal sampling unit for each user. The message is clear: demand is real, but where the device sits decides whether people accept it.

02Not at any price, so the first buyer is a public health agency

Business model

The survey was equally clear on price: more than two-thirds of respondents would not pay more than €5 for a complete test—the price of a coffee.

A cartridge containing freeze-dried reagents and precision optics cannot be manufactured at that price; that is not a pricing-strategy problem, it is the physical cost of the product. The first buyer must therefore be a public health agency, following the classic razor-and-blade model: readers are publicly funded and installed at fixed sites, cartridges are paid for per test, and health authorities subscribe to a real-time data dashboard. The development budget is approximately €300,000.

The honest summary: an individual will not pay to know a day sooner—but a health system will pay to see an outbreak a week earlier.

03Who puts them where the risk is known and the bite is fresh

The survey's message—"where" matters as much as "whether"—defines the deployment strategy: not one device per household, but devices at the places where the risk is known and the bite is fresh. Three site types qualify: university campuses (dense, highly mobile populations), community clinics and pharmacies (symptomatic people already visit), and neighbourhoods under active health alert during the mosquito season. In each, testing requires no special trip—it happens while studying, collecting a prescription, or walking home.

The top survey concerns double as siting rules: hygiene → single-use cartridges; vandalism → tamper-resistant wall mounting; "shared equipment" aversion → personal sterile samplers, with sharing limited to the reader you never touch. And deployment follows the risk: devices move in with the mosquito season and redeploy when it recedes—a fleet that travels with the outbreak, not machines left in place for years.

04First where the mosquito already lives, then wherever it arrives.

Expansion

The mosquito's map is being redrawn. Over the past thirty years, suitable habitat for Aedes mosquitoes has expanded roughly twenty-fold and continues to push toward higher latitudes (Liu-Helmersson et al.). France recorded its first locally acquired chikungunya cases in 2010; by 2025 the count had reached 809. The mosquito opens the corridor; the virus follows.

A-BITE expands in two phases. Phase one: the tropical and subtropical regions already living with both diseases, where fast diagnosis is needed most urgently. Phase two: as the mosquito advances, the newly exposed frontiers—Southern Europe and beyond—placing devices before the first local case appears, not after. The same logic extends the test menu: same reader, same cartridge—only the dried reagent formula changes, opening the door to Zika, West Nile and yellow fever without new hardware.

Note: only chikungunya and dengue are addressed in this project.

05What the network sells is speed, never identity.

Data & ethics

"Testing-as-surveillance" carries one non-negotiable rule: the data must be irreversibly separated from the person it came from. A single result is a medical fact; thousands of results are an outbreak map—and a legal and technical firewall must stand between the two.

A-BITE builds this firewall in four layers: separate consent for testing and for surveillance use (declining one never blocks the other); no names or identifiers recorded—only time, place and result; irreversible anonymisation before any aggregation; and minimum reporting thresholds, so that an area with very few tests produces no output at all. The entire system will undergo a Data Protection Impact Assessment under the French CNIL framework. What this network sells is exactly one thing: a faster, finer outbreak signal—never a named record.
