01A bite deposits far more virus than we assumed

Virus dose

A mosquito does not simply draw blood when it bites. It probes the skin repeatedly, injecting saliva each time—and if the mosquito carries a virus, that saliva is loaded with large numbers of infectious viral particles.

For years, the dose delivered by a single bite could only be estimated indirectly. Styer et al. (2007) measured it directly from mosquito saliva and found that one bite can deposit tens of thousands of infectious particles into the skin—far more than earlier estimates. The bite site is therefore not just an entry point: within minutes, it becomes a concentrated local reservoir of virus. The best place to detect the virus early is the bite site itself, not the blood. This insight is the starting point of A-BITE.

Ref. · Styer 2007

02The skin starts replicating it within hours

Early replication

The skin was long treated as a passive barrier: the virus enters, crosses into the blood, and only then begins to multiply. Tang et al. (2012) turned this timeline on its head. The skin is full of living cells that dengue and chikungunya viruses can infect—and within hours of a bite, the virus begins multiplying in them. The bite site becomes the body's first virus-producing factory.

In other words, the virus is already active locally a day or more before it can be found in the blood. Waiting for a blood test means missing the earliest window; sampling the skin at the bite site means catching the virus exactly where and when it is most concentrated. A-BITE's sampling strategy is a direct engineering answer to this skin-first biology.

Ref. · Tang 2012

03Microneedles can reach that fluid without drawing blood

Microneedle access

Just beneath the skin surface lies the interstitial fluid—the liquid that fills the spaces between cells. When an infection begins nearby, this fluid can carry viral particles and viral RNA. Unlike blood, it can be reached without puncturing a vessel.

Microneedles make this practical. An array of needles less than a millimetre long passes through the outer skin layers but stops above the blood vessels and pain nerves—the procedure is painless and blood-free (Al Sulaiman et al., 2019). A-BITE needs only a few microlitres of fluid, collected by a small patch in under 25 minutes. No syringe, no sharps waste, no professional required—sampling becomes as simple as applying a patch.

Ref. · Al Sulaiman 2019

04We already know how to read it

Reading the RNA

Once the fluid is collected, the device must answer one question quickly: does it contain dengue or chikungunya RNA? A-BITE's answer is RT-LAMP—a molecular test that, unlike PCR, runs at a single constant temperature and needs no repeated heating cycles.

That one simplification changes the hardware completely: a small heater, an LED and a light sensor are enough to amplify a few RNA copies a billion-fold and read the result within 20–40 minutes. Researchers have already demonstrated the full chain—reagents freeze-dried into a disposable tube with no refrigeration (Yaren 2017), detection built into a 3D-printed box (Priye 2017). In short: the chemistry that turns a drop of skin fluid into a clear yes/no answer already exists, and the instrument can fit in a hand.

Ref. · Yaren 2017; Priye 2017; Ribeiro da Silva 2024; Prado 2024

05The fluid under the bite

The link no one has measured

The four links now seem complete: a bite delivers a large viral dose; the skin amplifies it within hours; microneedles can reach that fluid without blood; and a portable test can read the RNA. But one link is missing—no published study has ever connected them in a single system.

The open question is precise: in the fluid directly beneath a mosquito bite, how much viral RNA appears, and how early? Viral RNA has been measured in blood, saliva and urine—but never in the bite-site fluid where it logically appears first. A-BITE's laboratory work is designed to take exactly this measurement. The answer will decide whether the entire "testing-as-surveillance" system is viable—it is the project's most fundamental scientific checkpoint.

No published study · INOVACHIK 2022; Viruses 2024: blood, saliva, urine only (to cite)
